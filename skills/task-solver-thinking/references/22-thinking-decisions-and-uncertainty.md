# Thinking cards: decisions and uncertainty

Read the selected card only. Begin with the decision owner, options, deadline, and hard constraints. Keep preferences, probabilities, estimates, and measurements separate. A numerical score must not turn missing evidence into false precision.

## 09. Decision Matrix

Use for options that can be compared on shared criteria. Eliminate infeasible options first. Define the meaning and direction of each score before scoring; use weights agreed by the decision owner. Include an appropriate baseline or doing nothing. Avoid overlapping criteria that count the same benefit twice.

**Artifact and test:** An option-by-criterion table with evidence and ranges. Change plausible weights and uncertain inputs: does the winner change? If it does, identify the preference or fact that controls the decision rather than reporting a definitive ranking. Fixed mandatory requirements remain gates, not low-weight disadvantages.

## 10. Eisenhower Matrix

Use when incoming urgency displaces important work. Define importance against an actual objective and urgency against a real deadline or consequence of delay. Classify the work, schedule important nonurgent actions, and identify urgent work that an authorized owner can handle. Remove work only within the user's scope.

**Artifact and test:** A short do/schedule/assign/defer list with owners and dates. Check dependencies: a small administrative prerequisite may be important because it unlocks delivery. If every task appears urgent, verify deadlines and workload rather than forcing a balanced quadrant count. The matrix itself grants no authority to delegate.

## 11. Impact-Effort Matrix

Use to reduce a backlog that exceeds capacity. Estimate benefit against one agreed outcome and effort including integration, verification, and maintenance. Show confidence and dependency risk beside each item. Separate a small discovery experiment from the larger implementation it might justify.

**Artifact and test:** A ranked shortlist with a cut line for available capacity. Recheck items near that line under pessimistic effort and benefit assumptions. Example: measuring a bottleneck may outrank replacing a service. Do not interpret a qualitative quadrant or impact/effort ratio as measured return; shared prerequisites can change the ordering.

## 12. Hard Choice Model

Use to decide how much analysis a choice deserves. Assess consequence, duration, reversibility, and whether alternatives share meaningful criteria. Low-impact choices deserve a timebox. High-impact comparable options deserve evidence. High-impact options reflecting different values need those values made explicit, possibly with trials that preserve options.

**Artifact and test:** A decision approach, evidence budget, stopping rule, and owner. Check whether more research could actually change the choice or whether the remaining disagreement is a preference. Do not force incomparable goals onto a synthetic score merely to manufacture an objective winner.

## 13. Confidence Determines Speed vs. Quality

Use when choosing between discovery work and a durable implementation. Evaluate evidence that the problem matters separately from evidence that the solution works. Weak problem evidence favors a cheap, honest probe. Strong problem evidence with uncertain solution calls for representative prototypes. Strong evidence for both can justify deeper reliability and usability work.

**Artifact and test:** An evidence table, intended build depth, and the observation that would justify further investment. A working prototype tests feasibility, not necessarily demand. Privacy, data integrity, and mandatory acceptance criteria remain intact at every depth; uncertainty is a reason to reduce scope, not bypass them.

## 14. Pareto Analysis

Use when measured contributors can explain a substantial share of an outcome. Choose the measurement window and unit, assign nonoverlapping categories, and rank their contributions. Calculate cumulative shares from the actual data; the distribution need not be 80/20. Keep severity separate from frequency where harm matters.

**Artifact and test:** A contribution table and the highest-value investigable category. Check missing records and whether the window is representative. Then measure whether the proposed intervention reduces the total outcome. A frequent category can be expensive to change; rare catastrophic failures may still take priority.

## 15. OODA Loop

Use for decisions that can be updated from timely feedback. Observe a relevant change, interpret it against the current goal and constraints, choose one bounded action, and execute within existing authorization. Set the next observation time and a stop condition before acting.

**Artifact and test:** Observation → interpretation → action → expected signal, with a timestamp. Compare the signal with the prediction before repeating. A shorter loop is useful only if observations are informative; repeatedly reacting to noisy metrics can destabilize the system. Irreversible actions need their own adequate evaluation before entering the loop.

## 16. Second-Order Thinking

Use when an immediate improvement may change later behavior or move cost elsewhere. Trace a few plausible consequences over relevant time horizons and across affected parties. Include incentives, adaptation, delayed costs, and the baseline path if nothing changes. Mark speculation and avoid branching indefinitely.

**Artifact and test:** A consequence chain with leading indicators, guardrails, and review dates. Example: simplifying sign-up may increase registrations but also support demand or low-quality usage. Measure the downstream outcome alongside the initial metric. Stop when the consequences can no longer affect the present decision or lack a testable mechanism.
